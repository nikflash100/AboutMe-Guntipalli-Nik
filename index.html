Projects

Signal

Adaptive diagnostic app for K–12

Signal diagnoses a student instead of guessing. It doesn't throw a pile of random courses at you, it works out what you actually need and gives you that.

It opens with a 16-question screen across a bunch of topics, then narrows with a staircase algorithm and a re-check pass until it lands on one weak spot. Five model calls end to end.

Most of the work went into making it not break. There's a 40-question offline bank for when the API is down, state that survives a force-close, streak tracking, and routing by grade band.

Thunkable · OpenAI gpt-4o-mini · Shown at the Spark Civics Civic Innovation Showcase

SuccessForecast

Predicting student outcomes

I compared 8 classifiers on a dataset of 2,392 students with 5-fold cross-validation, tuned the top three with grid search, and combined them into a soft-voting ensemble. A linear SVM came out ahead: 90.2% accuracy and 0.964 ROC-AUC on the test set.

The model wasn't the interesting part. Absences turned out to carry 62% of the predictive weight, more than study time, parental support, and parental education combined. I also ran K-Means on the same data and got four student archetypes with clearly different pass rates.

Python · scikit-learn · pandas · matplotlib · seaborn

From Failure to Flourishing

Exploratory analysis of student performance

I went through the same student dataset question by question, sixteen of them, looking for what actually separates failing students from passing ones. Absences were the clearest split: failing students averaged 19, passing students 7.5.

Most of what people assume matters didn't. Extracurricular participation moved the failure rate by about five points. Gender and ethnicity barely moved it at all.

Book Reader

Text analysis, written twice

Sixteen questions about a book: word and sentence counts, palindromes, anagrams, letter frequency, longest words, most common words with stopwords filtered out. I wrote it once as a Python notebook and once as an object-oriented Java class.

Writing the same thing twice made the differences concrete in a way reading about them didn't. The Java version also taught me something the Python one didn't: I was grouping every occurrence of a word instead of every distinct word, so common words came back as anagrams of themselves.
